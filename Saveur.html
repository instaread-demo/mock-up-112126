How a Jungle Farm in the Yucatán Is Transforming Its Maya Community
The Mestiza de Indias agricultural collective is using the ancient regenerative concept of the milpa to reshape the local way of life.

By Shane Mitchell

Published on November 5, 2026

“The future is ancestral,” said Gonzalo Samaranch Granados. “Mayans were very good observers of the sky, but also the jungle and its complex relationship of plants and animals.” The rainy season, when farming deep in the Yucatán jungle proves more challenging than usual, had ended, and he was preparing for the next growing cycle, when the garden beds at Mestiza de Indias, a 550-acre reserve and biodiversity lab, would once again sprout heirloom tomatoes, eggplant, carrots, kale, garlic, herbs—crops popular with restaurant and chef collaborators on the Riviera Maya—as well as indigenous varieties with deep cultural significance for the local Mayan community. 

Mak’ulan (pepperleaf), pepitas, and maize form the base of the kots’ob tamales prepared by market stall women in the nearby town of Espita. Cempasúchil (marigolds) decorate altars during Día de Muertos. Tomatillos milperos (purple tomatillos) introduced from Hidalgo are added to sikil p’aak and sopa de lima. Samaranch selects and saves native seeds, but he also sources from organic seed companies such as Baker’s Creek and Kitazawa. He’s currently on the hunt for wild ts’unu’un p’aak, or hummingbird tomatoes, a variety endemic to the Yucatán.

Other passion projects at Mestiza de Indias include the cultivation of rare Maya vanilla orchids and the extraction of a citrusy honey produced by endangered Melipona bees. (Ancient Mayans were avid apiarists. Ah Muzen Kab, the Maya god of bees, is featured in the 1,100-year-old Madrid Codex, which also contains detailed instructions for beekeeping.) Next to a ts’iits’il che’ tree where orchid vines twined, Samaranch walked into a screened enclosure protecting square wood boxes that serve as hives for these stingless bees. He removed the top of one to reveal treasure: Instead of combs, the bees create waxen pods, oozing amber-hued honey. It’s never a lot, so every precious taste is bittersweet. Equally important, they are critical pollinators for tropical ecosystems and go crazy for the nectar of ts’iits’il che’ blossoms, which flower after the first rainfall after the dry season. “What we do is possible because I was fascinated by traditional Mayan agriculture,” said Samaranch, a native of Barcelona who traded the art world for regenerative farming. “It’s not only making food. This system is related to culture, medicine, spirituality. It’s about living in harmony with nature, a respectful system.”

This Mesoamerican worldview is known as the milpa. In the Yucatán, it refers to the symbiotic planting of corn, beans, and squashes, often called the Three Sisters, on small-acre farms. (Other Indigenous growers consider sunflowers or bee balm the fourth sister. And when watermelon was introduced to the New World in the 16th century, it was adopted as a sister, too.) The concept of caring for the land, putting back more than you take, replenishing waterways, and reviving heritage crops manifests differently across continents and climates, but it exists wherever farming communities still observe old and slow ways.

By adapting these polyculture techniques into a regenerative project, Samaranch is transforming both the land and its people, including the way they look at the culture of food itself. And he’s not alone: In Australia’s Far East Gippsland, Bruce Pascoe founded Black Duck Foods to perpetuate Aboriginal land-management traditions. Black Duck: A Year at Yumburra is his manifesto about caring for country but also a personal reclamation after devastating bushfires and the loss of elders.

At Royal Queen Farms in Acadia Parish, Louisiana, artist and land steward Michelle Joan Papillion has created a food sovereignty network for her community while protecting species native to the region. Johnny Ortiz-Concha and his wife, Maida Branch, bring people together around a communal table at /shed, a subscription supper club that consciously celebrates their ancestry and ecosystem. Menus are based on the crops and livestock they raise or forage at their ranch outside Taos Pueblo. They are custodians of a landrace variety of blue corn known as Tu-ah-tah, gifted to the couple by Ortiz-Concha’s family.

For those who work at Mestiza des Indias, change also starts close to home. The reserve lies almost four miles down a rough track outside the Mayan village of San Pedro Chenchelá, where most residents still live in primitive casa de huano, or mud-daubed wooden houses thatched with palm, and wear brightly patterned huipils, the distinctive Mayan tunic that predates Hispanic fashions. Hiking on foot to the milpa, field-workers have to beware of jaguars, rattlesnakes, and even poisonous trees. 

During high season, Samaranch presently employs about 10 workers, most of them women whose husbands commute to the coast for tourism-related jobs. Others leave for bigger cities. Because family members can be away from home for extended periods, this has disrupted the ancestral rhythm of life. And when they do return to the community, it’s often with addiction problems. Even deep in the Yucatán jungle, the arrival of methamphetamine has devastated the region’s younger generation. 

To counter this cultural disruption, Samaranch’s Mayan wife, Martha Elena Chan Tuz, a community project leader and former municipal tourism secretary, launched a complementary initiative, forming a cooperative of local needlework crafters who hand embroider table linens with intricate xookbil chuy, or cross-stitches. Their exquisite dinner napkins appear on tables during a communal meal in La Ceiba Garden & Kitchen at Rosewood Mayakoba. “Bien contentas,” said Chan Tuz, when asked how her neighbors have responded to the project. Samaranch explained: “It gives the women some independence. They have money and can manage their time. It’s something positive and very helpful, a job they can combine with what they do at home. Normally, they have to ask permission to work with us, but in this case, the men are grateful the women can contribute.”

Each worker gets to take home a basket of produce from the milpa as part of their wages. With only modest tiendas that stock junk food and shelf-stable canned goods, it’s generally a struggle to put fresh, healthy food on the table. (Especially when backyard milpas go untended because breadwinners are absent.) “We thought the people who grow the food should eat the food,” said Samaranch. “They get the same as the resorts we supply, so we have changed the diet.” He explained that children in San Pedro Chenchelá and the neighboring village of Kunché are now more excited about vegetables than soda or chips. “When a kid asks, ‘When will the eggplants be ready again?’ I feel we’re doing right.”

Where’d You Go, Crêpes Suzette?

This flame-kissed French classic is ready for a 21st-century renaissance.

  • Culture
  • Features
  • Trends

By Ellery Weil

Published on November 16, 2026

ADVERTISEMENTAD
AD

By Ellery Weil

Published on November 16, 2026

There’s something simultaneously comforting and dazzling about setting a dessert on fire: Candles atop a birthday cake; the meringue encrusting a baked Alaska; the caramelized fruit in a classic bananas Foster. But chief among them is one whose very name promises something different, something with French sophistication and an air of mystery that demands a moniker all its own: crêpes Suzette.

Crêpes Suzette is a surprisingly simple dish considering its decadent, theatrical reputation. The drama comes from the flames—thin, traditional French crêpes are drenched in a sauce made of orange liqueur, which is then set ablaze (typically tableside in many restaurants), causing the spirits to simmer and the sugars to caramelize. French chef Andrea Calstier, of Michelin-starred La Bastide in North Salem, New York, grew up eating them with his family, and in the brasseries of his home country.

For such a famous dish, though, its appearance on restaurant menus is increasingly rare. And for such an iconic French dessert, well, it may not technically be French at all. So where did these flaming pancakes come from? Where did they go? And isn’t it about time we bring them back?

While there are conflicting reports about the original creator of crêpes Suzette, the top candidate is Henri Charpentier, chef at the Café de Paris in Monte Carlo, Monaco, in the late 1800s. But while multiple roads lead back to Charpentier, the path gets murky, contradicted by both general plausibility and Charpentier himself.

Originally, Charpentier claimed credit for the iconic dish in his autobiography, Life à la Henri: Being the Memoirs of Henri Charpentier. In this 1934 volume, he suggested that the dish was a happy accident, tinged with drama. He alleged that as a young man working at the Café de Paris, he had been carrying a platter of crêpes to the Prince of Wales (the future King Edward VII) and stepped too near a chafing dish, setting the crêpes on fire, to the shock and delight of the prince, who proposed naming the newly created dish after his dining companion, a young woman named Suzette. (Frustratingly little information is available on who this Suzette may have been, if she was a real person at all.)

AD

The story has the right theatrical touches for the dish, but it is almost certainly apocryphal, not least because Charpentier himself, in an interview with American writer Elsie Lee for the 1950s “Compleat Imbiber” series, said that he had developed the dish by taking a beloved childhood fruit crêpe and adding the then-fashionable touches of a liquor-based sauce and, presumably, a flame.

Wherever it came from, though, and whatever Charpentier had to say about it, Suzette’s unwitting legacy has endured for more than a century—her crêpes have remained a household name, even if they have fallen somewhat out of style. While chef Calstier notes that they’re still “served in many restaurants” in his native Marseille and that “French customers…are accustomed to seeing crêpes Suzette in any brasserie,” he admitted that “here in the U.S., they are more difficult to find.”

Part of the reason may be that, as with many dishes that predate the Edwardian era, it simply went out of fashion. But according to Lucile Plaza, executive chef at Benoit New York, it’s a result of the dish’s inherent service requirements: “True tableside service requires high staffing levels and precise choreography that a busy modern kitchen can’t always support daily,” she says. In London’s West End, fine-dining heavyweights Savoy Grill and The Ritz take this showmanship to the extreme, with portable burners and bottles of Grand Marnier on wooden trolleys. 

But while there are complications to adding an edible spectacle to a restaurant menu, there are benefits, too. Chef Plaza notes that for the kitchen, “Suzette is brilliant because it is the ultimate way to use leftover crêpes. The intense bathing in the hot, rich citrus sauce revives the starch perfectly.” But not every restaurant that serves crêpes Suzette does so with a tableside flourish: In London’s St. John’s Wood, Oslo Court Restaurant, which is famous for its retro menu of classics like steak Diane and sole Veronique (two more of Edward VII's girlfriends, perhaps?), crêpes Suzette are brûléed with a torch in the kitchen rather than set aflame at the table. Adding crêpes Suzette to a menu may only be as hard as a restaurant wants it to be.

A burst of blue-tinged flame may not be necessary for the dish to achieve its signature caramelization, but it is key to the show—and a major point in favor of the dish’s present-day revival. As the old saying goes, “we eat with our eyes first,” and it’s truer than ever today. In fact, for many diners in the age of social media, it’s the phone that eats first, and that’s where crêpes Suzette has real potential for a modern renaissance. Nearly 400,000 viewers have watched a YouTube video of the tableside crêpes Suzette service at the London Ritz, and more than a million have viewed a TikTok of crêpes Suzette being prepared tableside at Switzerland’s five-star Suvretta House in Saint Moritz.

AD

Spectacle is cool again, and whether dining out or cooking at home, we want to feel astonished and excited by the foods we eat. While a tableside flambé may prove logistically complicated at a restaurant scale, the feat is significantly easier to pull off at home—all it takes is a lighter or a match and a little confidence. Chef Calstier, for one, says that crêpes Suzette are a favorite of his wife, Elena, adding, “You can prepare everything in advance and do the flambé in front of your guests to wow them.” Plus, he says, “they don’t require many ingredients, beyond that bottle of Grand Marnier that we all have stashed away somewhere. They’re quite simple to make.”

Moreover, as delectable as classic crêpes Suzette are, they lend themselves well to new takes and customizations. Chef Plaza’s version at Benoit has a cherry twist: “Replacing Grand Marnier with kirsch is a natural match,” she says. “The dry cherry distillate brings an incredible aroma to the tableside without adding cloying sugar.” Meanwhile, at La Bastide, chef Calstier dishes up a twist that uses French toast instead of crêpes while sticking to the classic flavors and flambé. We asked one of our favorite pastry chefs, Caroline Schiff, author of Daily Desserts: Treats on Repeat, to develop a savory version for us, which she describes as “a cheeky little twist” on the classic. In her take, inspired by French onion soup, crêpes layered with caramelized onions and nutty gruyère are set ablaze with a splash of sherry. “They are at once a novelty and a nod to the classic,” she says.

Charpentier, one imagines, would approve.
